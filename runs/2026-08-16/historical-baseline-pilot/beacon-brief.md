# Beacon Choice Brief — historical-baseline pilot

**Status:** draft method pilot; not for publication and not a live-condition brief.  
**Scope:** three US historical comparisons; sources accessed 2026-08-16.

## 1. The condition

This pilot examines a recurring condition: a tool takes over bounded, routine work and organisations redistribute tasks around it. It does not claim that contemporary AI is the same condition.

## 2. The evidence terrain

The record contains one observed diffusion series and six graded research or signal records. US Census makes a computer-use series from 1984–2009 available, which establishes diffusion context but not workplace causality ([Census](https://www.census.gov/data/tables/time-series/demo/computer-internet/computer-use-1984-2009.html)).

For office work, BLS reports an 80.4% fall in employment of word processors and typists from 1999–2018, while explicitly warning that offshoring complicates causal attribution ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2022/article/growth-trends-for-selected-occupations-considered-at-risk-from-automation.htm)). Research using US task data associates computerization with reduced relative demand for routine tasks and increased demand for non-routine cognitive tasks ([Autor, Levy & Murnane](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8337)). A later US office-support study associates software adoption with broader tasks and higher listed skills, with gains concentrated among college-educated women in its setting ([Dillender & Forsythe](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29866)).

The banking counter-case is that ATM task automation coincided with expanded branch networks and altered teller work in the cited study's account ([Bessen](https://www.bu.edu/law/files/2015/11/NewTech-2.pdf)). In contrast, robot exposure was associated with lower employment and wages in exposed US commuting zones from 1990–2007 ([Acemoglu & Restrepo](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285)).

## 3. The historical rhyme

The rhyme is not that every new tool repeats these outcomes. It is that task-level change is a poor shortcut for judging occupational, local, or institutional consequences. See [history.md](history.md) for where each analogy breaks and what it must not license.

## 4. The human question

Before a new work tool becomes a default, who can see which tasks are changing; who receives the newly valued work; whose entry route narrows; and who carries error-checking, exception handling, and recourse?

## 5. The choice terrain

Possible terrain for a future live condition includes:

- **Learn:** whether people can gain access to the task knowledge that remains or becomes newly valued.
- **Protect:** whether time, job quality, entry routes, and the right to question a result need safeguarding.
- **Redesign:** whether teams can deliberately allocate exceptions, review, and customer-facing judgement rather than treating them as leftovers.
- **Contest:** whether workers and affected communities have a voice in defaults, metrics, and distribution of gains.
- **Wait:** whether evidence is too thin to normalise a tool or a policy response.

These are response types, not recommendations to a reader.

## 6. Counter-case and uncertainty

The ATM case cautions against an automatic displacement story; the robot evidence cautions against an automatic augmentation story. The baseline is US-heavy, has no participant testimony, and does not establish what people experienced during transitions. It cannot speak for the middle 60% without live, situated inquiry.
