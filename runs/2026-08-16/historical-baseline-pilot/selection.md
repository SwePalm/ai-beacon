# Selection — historical-baseline pilot

## Pilot condition

**When a tool takes over bounded, routine parts of work, how are tasks, entry routes, responsibility, and bargaining power redistributed?**

This is a historical baseline, not Beacon's first live AI condition. It is a deliberately small method test using three US cases with distinct outcomes:

1. automated teller machines (retail banking, principally 1970s onward);
2. office computerization (1960–98 and 2007–16 research windows); and
3. industrial robots (US local labour markets, 1990–2007).

## Linked themes

`work`, `learning`, `judgment`, `agency`, and `power`.

## Why it may matter to ordinary people

Each case concerns work that is widespread or an important route into wider participation. The baseline tests whether Beacon can keep a change in *tasks* separate from a claim about whole occupations, and whether it can show different effects across people and places.

## Initial question

What questions should a future live-condition brief ask before it treats a tool as either displacement or augmentation?

## Counter-interpretations to retain

- Task substitution can coexist with employment growth when organisations expand services or demand.
- Aggregate employment can obscure losses concentrated in particular places, firms, or entry-level roles.
- Observed change can reflect trade, organisational design, education, regulation, and demand alongside a tool.
- These cases concern earlier technologies and US evidence; none establishes an outcome for contemporary AI.

## Exclusions

- No claim about the prevalence, net effect, or desirability of current AI.
- No ranking of occupations by “risk.”
- No recommendation to retrain, adopt, resist, or regulate.
- No use of a historical parallel as a prediction.
