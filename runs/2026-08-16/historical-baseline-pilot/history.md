# Historical comparison

## Comparator 1: retail banking and ATMs

**Similarity.** A bounded, repeatable transaction moved from a worker-facing interaction to a machine. The comparison asks whether the unit of analysis should be the task, the branch, the occupation, or the local service system.

**Difference.** ATMs handle a narrow transaction in a regulated, place-based service model. They are not a comparison for systems that generate text, make classifications, or operate across many functions.

**Affected groups visible in the evidence.** Tellers and branch staff are most visible; customers and workers excluded from service redesign are less visible in this small record.

**Evidence limits.** The baseline relies on retrospective research synthesis rather than a new archival series of branch, hiring, and wage data.

**Lesson not to import.** Do not import “automation creates more jobs” from the ATM story. Organisational expansion is a contingent mechanism, not a technological law.

## Comparator 2: office computerization and clerical work

**Similarity.** General-purpose office tools can redistribute routine formatting, filing, calculation, and coordination tasks, and can alter what employers list as entry requirements.

**Difference.** The underlying research spans several technologies and periods. It cannot tell us whether a particular future tool changes responsibility, error liability, or managerial control in the same way.

**Affected groups visible in the evidence.** Office-support workers, applicants, and workers with different educational access are visible. Informal work, household effects, and workers outside the US are not adequately represented.

**Evidence limits.** A decline in a named occupation is not an account of what happened to every person, and BLS cautions that offshoring and other forces complicate attribution.

**Lesson not to import.** Do not equate “higher stated skills” with better work, broader opportunity, or a fairer entry route.

## Comparator 3: industrial robots and local labour markets

**Similarity.** Technology adoption can be concentrated by industry and place, producing consequences that a national or firm-level average conceals.

**Difference.** Industrial robots work in physical production under capital-intensive operating models; this is not an analogue for every knowledge-work tool.

**Affected groups visible in the evidence.** Workers and wages in robot-exposed US commuting zones are visible. The study does not identify every household or institutional response.

**Evidence limits.** The estimates are local to the 1990–2007 US setting and should not be read as a universal robot effect.

**Lesson not to import.** Do not use a locally negative estimate to assert inevitable aggregate displacement—or to erase the distributional harm that a positive aggregate story can hide.

## Baseline comparison

The three cases disagree with a one-direction story. They support a more demanding future inquiry: map the task, the organisation's demand response, the distribution across people and places, and the institutions that allocate gains, verification duties, and recourse. This is a comparison frame, not a prediction about AI.
