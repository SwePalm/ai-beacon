## What changed, what did not, and what remains uncertain

The monthly material makes one limited movement clearer: in a live field setting,
human involvement after agent failure is not a generic final check. The Taobao
study reported different recovery effects by escalation type, timing, and the
worker's post-escalation effort. This is a useful counterweight to treating
“human in the loop” as a complete description of a workflow.

The month does **not** establish that persistent agents have broadly changed
ordinary work, that one governance design works across settings, or that product
and regulatory change has yet produced measurable everyday effects. US material
was provider-authored; EU material mixed an official enforcement change with a
self-described adoption case; the strongest workflow study was a China-based
preprint on one platform. The reported Chinese worker accounts are important
testimony, but cannot identify prevalence or causation.

## Signal lifecycle decisions

No formal signal record or readiness hypothesis is due for review before
2026-11-16, so none is strengthened, weakened, contradicted, or expired in the
append-only state. The following are retained as **questions for collection**,
not lifecycle decisions:

- Does the quality of handoff information, authority, and available time shape
  whether human recovery is meaningful?
- When work is delegated, who absorbs the exception and emotional-repair burden?
- Do transparency and governance requirements lead to usable recourse or merely
  additional compliance work?

## Capability terrain

| Locus | Conditional capacity becoming more visible | What the August material does not justify |
|---|---|---|
| Person | Diagnose a failure, judge whether to intervene, and recover a situated interaction. | A demand that every person become an agent operator or take personal liability for system design. |
| Team | Make handoff triggers, escalation routes, review time, and responsibility visible and shared. | A universal team process or the assumption that more approvals are better. |
| Institution | Arrange permissions, traceability, observability, resourcing, and recourse around delegated work. | Treating compliance artefacts or supplier controls as proof of meaningful accountability. |
| Public system | Set enforceable transparency, worker protection, and complaint/recourse conditions. | Claiming that the EU's enforcement start has already changed everyday conditions. |

## Collection bias and gaps

- Product and provider sources remained much easier to find than worker or
  affected-person accounts.
- The three-centre lens was useful for avoiding a US-only story, but the sources
  were non-equivalent: EU official policy, US provider analysis, China supplier
  infrastructure, one China field experiment, and reported China testimony.
- There was no independent US workflow trace and no EU account of transparency
  enforcement changing a live workflow.
- English-language collection, formal organisations, knowledge work, and
  professional sources remain over-represented. Small organisations, public
  services, labour representation, informal work, and downstream affected people
  are under-represented.

## Next learning move

Run a narrow watch on **handoff and recovery in delegated AI work**. Seek two or
more bounded, attributable workflow accounts in different settings—preferably
including a non-technical US or EU setting—and record: the delegated task; the
handoff trigger; what the human receives; time and authority to intervene; and
what happens after a failed recovery.

## Decision

`narrow watch`

Do not start a new full pipeline run yet. The first month has generated a
testable, more concrete question, but not enough diverse evidence to support a
new Choice Brief or a method change. A later review may reasonably decide to
pause or archive the experiment if the narrow watch continues to yield only
product claims rather than usable learning.
